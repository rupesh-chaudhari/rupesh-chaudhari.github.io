#5

The return trip: the supply chain nobody plans

Reverse logistics, across industries
Read

What the data says

~USD 35 bnIndia reverse logistics market, 2025; forecast to more than double by 2031
₹1,900 cryearly reverse-handling cost GS1 India links to poor product data
95%+returnable beer bottles collected in Japan, about 3 trips a year each

Reverse flow is no longer a side process. India’s reverse logistics market was estimated at about USD 35 billion in 2025, with transportation the largest cost block at roughly half the market. GS1 India estimates poor product data costs the sector close to ₹5,000 crore a year, about ₹1,900 crore of it in reverse handling and processing.

  • Quick commerce: industry estimates put grocery and FMCG return rates at 6–12%, reverse pickups at ₹60–120 an order, and packaging damage at nearly a fifth of returns in snacks, beverages and cosmetics. Refunds go out in minutes; inspection and stock reconciliation at the dark store take far longer.
  • E-commerce: industry trackers put D2C return-to-origin (RTO) rates at roughly 20–35%, with cash-on-delivery orders far riskier than prepaid ones, and each failed parcel costing ₹150–300 in direct logistics. Blue Dart calls RTO a bigger margin problem than customer returns.
  • Breweries and returnable glass: United Breweries once met 75% of its bottling needs with second-hand bottles, with bottles making up 40% of its costs. Middlemen hoarding empties pushed used bottle prices up 30%, which is why brewers moved to embossed, company-specific bottles. Japan shows the other end: a ¥5 deposit, over 95% collected, each bottle back at the plant in about four months.
  • Packaged goods and regulation: the 2026 plastic waste amendment sets the first reuse targets for rigid plastic packaging (10% for 0.9–4.9 litre containers, 70% for large water packs, 10% for other large packs), alongside recycled content rising from 30% to 60% by 2028–29.
  • General trade FMCG and pharma: damaged and near-expiry stock comes back from distributors and retailers, usually settled through credit notes, then sits in a depot corner waiting for a decision.

How returnable bottle float grows as the return cycle slips

4-week cycle100
5-week cycle125
6-week cycle150
8-week cycle200

Bottles needed in the market, indexed to a 4-week cycle. Float ≈ weekly fills × cycle time in weeks. My illustration, not sourced data.

My take

Every company plans the forward flow down to the SKU and the day. The reverse flow gets handled, not planned. Four habits change that:

  • Forecast returns like demand. Empties coming back are past sales, delayed by the cycle time and reduced by the return rate. If the cycle slips from 4 weeks to 6, you need 50% more bottles in the market, usually right before peak season, when glass is hardest to buy.
  • Use the empty truck. Most forward trucks come back empty or part-loaded. Putting empties, crates and returns on the return leg is the cheapest reverse network there is.
  • Decide at first touch. Resell, rework, liquidate or scrap should be decided where the item is first received. Every kilometre a scrap item travels to a central warehouse is pure loss.
  • Treat returns as a signal. Near-expiry returns are a forecasting or deployment error showing up late. Damage returns point to packaging or handling. RTOs point to address and order-validation gaps. Feed each back into the forward plan.
Your view: in your industry, who owns the return flow, and does anyone actually forecast it?

Sources: Research and Markets / Mordor, Jun 2026; Base, returns in quick commerce; ClickPost, RTO benchmarks 2026; Glassonline via British Glass, 2010; Japan Partnership for Circular Economy; Plastic Waste Management Rules 2026 summary.

#4

Onboard the AI agent like a trainee planner

Open discussion
Read

What the data says

83%of large firms surveyed have spent USD 3M+ automating supply chain planning
5%will make even 10% of planning decisions autonomously by 2030, per Gartner
~80%of consumer goods companies still run traditional or collaborative S&OP

The money is moving faster than the capability. In a Gartner survey of 243 senior leaders at large companies, 83% had spent at least USD 3 million on automating supply chain planning, yet Gartner predicts only 5% of organisations will make at least 10% of planning decisions autonomously by 2030. Its own view of where autonomy will come first is telling: routine decisions such as replenishment and order prioritisation, while network design and inventory policy stay with people.

Adoption of the features is still set to rise fast, from about 5% of companies using supply chain software in 2025 to 60% by 2030, with Gartner stressing human oversight in the early years. McKinsey finds around 80% of consumer goods companies still run traditional or collaborative S&OP, but the few further along report up to 20% less inventory, up to 10% lower supply chain cost and up to 4% more revenue. Meanwhile Gartner expects over 40% of agentic AI projects to be cancelled by the end of 2027 over cost, unclear value or weak controls.

The autonomy gap: spending vs. real autonomous decisions

Spent USD 3M+ on planning automation83%
Expected to use agentic AI features by 203060%
Will make 10%+ of planning decisions autonomously by 20305%

Gartner surveys and predictions, 2025–2026. Different bases, shown side by side to illustrate the gap.

My take

An agent can only automate a decision you can already write down. That’s why spending isn’t the bottleneck: most teams have never written down how they decide. So I’d onboard an agent the way I’d onboard a trainee planner.

  • Week one work first: daily deployment exceptions using allocation rules the business already agreed; order and PO triage where the agent flags, ranks and drafts and a planner approves; and master data checks on pack sizes, lead times and MOQs before errors reach the plan.
  • Keep with people for now: capacity trade-offs, co-packer allocation, network and inventory policy, and anything that changes a customer commitment.
  • Promote on evidence: recommend, then act with approval, then act within guardrails. Track the agent’s acceptance rate and the error rate of accepted actions, and move up a rung only after several weeks above an agreed threshold.
  • Measure what it frees up: if planners save two hours a day, decide in advance what that time goes to. Otherwise the saving disappears into more firefighting.
Your view: which planning decision in your team is rule-based enough to automate tomorrow, and which would you never hand over?

Sources: Gartner press release, Sep 2026; Gartner press release, Apr 2026; McKinsey, autonomous planning for consumer goods; Supply Chain Digest, Jul 2026; summary of Gartner’s agentic AI cancellation forecast.

#3

Digital twin or just a network model? A four-question test

Supply chain digitisation
Read

What the data says

60%of supply chain leaders piloting or planning a digital supply chain twin (Gartner, 2023)
29%of supply chain organisations have built the capabilities needed for the future (Gartner, 2025)
90%of supply chain leaders say they lack the talent for their digital goals (McKinsey)

Interest is high and follow-through is thin. In a Gartner survey of 380 supply chain leaders, 60% were piloting or planning a digital supply chain twin. Yet in a 2025 Gartner study, only 29% of supply chain organisations had built most of the capabilities they’ll need, and most leaders had not yet invested in real-time visibility or digital twins, planning to do so within three to five years. McKinsey adds the constraint that usually decides it: 90% of supply chain leaders say they lack the talent and skills for their digital goals.

The definition matters. The Digital Twin Consortium describes a twin as a virtual copy kept in sync with the real system at a set frequency and level of detail; McKinsey stresses that the live data link is what separates it from a one-off model. Reported gains include one OEM cutting freight and damage costs by 8%.

How supply chain leaders rate emerging tech as important and disruptive

Artificial intelligence52%
Digital supply chain twin40%
Digital twin of the customer27%

Gartner survey of 380 supply chain leaders, 2023.

My take

Most “digital twins” I’ve seen are network models built once for a project and never touched again. Before buying a platform, I’d ask four questions:

  • How often does it refresh with actuals? Yearly is a study. Monthly is a tool. Weekly starts to be a twin.
  • Which recurring decision does it inform? If you can’t name one, it’s a poster.
  • Who opens it every week? A twin with one expert user dies when that person leaves, which is exactly the talent gap McKinsey describes.
  • Does it learn? Compare what the model predicted with what happened, and fix the gaps. Forecast-versus-actual on cost per case and service by lane is the simplest scorecard.

In my experience, a network optimisation model refreshed monthly with ERP actuals and used in the S&OP meeting gets most of the value for a fraction of the cost. Build the habit first, then the platform.

Your view: does your company have a twin in use, or a model that was built once? What stopped it from becoming a habit?

Sources: SCMR on Gartner digital twin survey, 2023; Gartner press release, Feb 2025; o9, citing McKinsey surveys; Winter Simulation Conference tutorial, 2023; McKinsey on digital twins; Ryder, citing McKinsey.

India’s logistics cost is about 8%, not 14%. Now what?

Logistics improvement
Read

What the data says

7.97%of GDP: India’s first official logistics cost estimate, 2023–24 (~₹24 lakh crore)
~70%of India’s freight moves by road
37–38 km/haverage truck speed on highways, barely up from a decade ago

The first official estimate, by NCAER for DPIIT, puts India’s logistics cost at 7.97% of GDP for 2023–24, replacing the long-quoted 13–14%. Road accounts for about 42% of that cost, warehousing is the biggest cost outside transport, and the target is closer to 7% by 2030.

Rail is not the easy answer. Rail averages ₹1.96 per tonne-km only when first and last mile are left out, and heavy trailers already come in at ₹1.51. Rail wins only beyond about 600 km, or about 1,000 km once the road legs at each end reach 100 km.

The bigger gap is time. A government study found trucks average just 37–38 km/h on highways and 47–48 km/h on expressways, against 65–70 km/h in the US and China, and the road ministry has flagged loading and unloading delays as the reason. Utilisation is the other lever: in one 3PL case, concentrating trucks on lanes with two-way demand lifted utilisation from 68% to 82% and cut empty kilometres by 21%.

Average commercial vehicle speed (km/h)

India, highways37–38
India, expressways47–48
US and China65–70

Government study as reported in 2026. India’s roads are built for 100–120 km/h; the gap is mostly waiting, not driving.

My take

If the national gap is smaller than we thought, the wins won’t come from waiting for new corridors. They’ll come from inside our own networks:

  • Measure cost per case by lane, split into primary and secondary. National averages hide the few lanes that cost the most.
  • Fix the dock, not just the road. If a truck spends a day waiting at each end, a faster highway saves nothing. Track turnaround time at every plant and depot, and set loading slots.
  • Fill the truck before negotiating the rate. A truck running 80% full costs more per case than a dearer truck running full, and planning returns onto the back leg cuts empty kilometres.
  • Don’t assume rail is cheaper. Shunting and handling at both ends eat its rate advantage. For most FMCG lanes, which run under 600 km, a well-loaded truck wins.
  • Revisit the depot footprint every year. Demand moves faster than warehouse leases do.
Your view: does your company know its logistics cost as a share of sales by lane? If not, what’s the hardest number to get?

Sources: NCAER, Assessment of Logistics Cost in India, Sep 2025; Business Standard, Nov 2025; Business Today on rail vs road, Sep 2025; report on truck speeds and idle fleet, 2026; Nexdigm, road freight utilisation case.

#1

The missing weekly meeting: why S&OE matters as much as S&OP

Planning process improvement
Read

What the data says

10%of companies very satisfied with their S&OE (AIMMS survey)
5–20 ptshigher service levels at companies with mature planning (McKinsey)
10–15%lower freight and inventory costs at mature planners (McKinsey)

Gartner describes sales and operations execution (S&OE) as a separate, short-horizon process that carries out the agreed S&OP plan and handles near-term problems. The payoff from mature planning is well documented: McKinsey finds mature planners run service levels 5 to 20 percentage points higher with freight and inventory costs 10 to 15% lower, and Bain reports forecasts 20–50% more accurate and inventory 10–30% lower at companies that move beyond basic S&OP.

Yet few run S&OE well. In an AIMMS survey only 10% of respondents were very satisfied with their S&OE, and close to 40% ran it on spreadsheets. Gartner finds S&OE meetings lose people when they become order reviews and status tracking.

The S&OE gap

Run S&OE on spreadsheets~40%
Very satisfied with their S&OE10%

AIMMS S&OE survey, 2021.

My take

Most companies already hold this meeting. They just call it the daily or weekly review, and in practice two things go wrong.

It turns into firefighting for availability. The agenda shrinks to which SKUs are short and where. Planners spend most of their time explaining connectivity: where the stock is, what’s in transit and why it isn’t reaching the market. That leaves little time to decide what to change.

The planning team becomes the face of it, and accountability fades. In most organisations, other departments stop attending because planning “owns” the meeting. Planning ends up answering for production misses, sales spikes and transport delays it doesn’t control, and decisions don’t stick because the people who must act on them weren’t in the room.

What helps:

  • Fix the horizon at roughly the next 4 to 13 weeks. Anything beyond goes to S&OP; anything inside a week goes to the daily call.
  • Make visibility self-serve. A shared stock and in-transit dashboard everyone reads before the meeting, so no meeting time goes on explaining where stock is.
  • Bring only exceptions above an agreed threshold, each with an owner and two options.
  • Let each function present its own exceptions: production for line misses, sales for demand changes, logistics for transit delays. Planning facilitates rather than answering for everyone.
  • Measure the meeting itself: share of time on decisions versus status (aim for status under a third), decisions closed on time, plan adherence week on week, and attendance by function. If those numbers don’t move, the meeting isn’t working.
Your view: who should own S&OE in a consumer goods business: planning, operations or sales?

Sources: AIMMS S&OE survey, 2021; Gartner, best practices for S&OE meetings, 2025; o9, citing McKinsey; Bain, beyond S&OP; River Logic, citing Gartner.

From the field

Problems I’ve worked on that shaped how I think about planning. Companies are kept anonymous; the lessons are the point.

Network and capacity planning for a national bottler

20 plant and co-packer sites, 35 distribution centres, ~400M cases a year

Problem
The network was running close to capacity ahead of peak season. The business needed to decide where to source each product, how much to give co-packers, and when and where to add capacity.
Approach
Built network optimization models (MIP) in Supply Chain Guru and Python covering sourcing, co-pack allocation and greenfield and brownfield expansion. Ran scenarios with finance and operations to agree the plan.
Outcome
₹27 Cr in savings, a 2.35% cut in supply chain cost, and +2% asset utilization, plus an agreed capacity expansion roadmap.
Lesson
A network model earns trust when finance and operations help build the scenarios, not when the answer is mathematically optimal.

Planning system rollout and centre of excellence

Blue Yonder APS, national scope

Problem
Planning ran on spreadsheets and varied by region, so plans were slow to build and hard to compare.
Approach
Led a five-module Blue Yonder deployment including MRP (SCS, ESP, SEQ, IO and DRP models). Redesigned the planning process and trained planners across regions.
Outcome
One planning system in use nationally and 12 process improvements put into practice.
Lesson
Software goes live in months; planning habits take a year. Redesign the process first, then the screens.

Regional supply planning

140+ SKUs, multi-plant region

Problem
Frequent stock-outs and high freight costs across a large, seasonal product range.
Approach
Reset inventory norms and the deployment plan, and optimized the transport and 3PL network.
Outcome
95%+ service level, 30% fewer stock-outs and ₹2.5 Cr in freight savings.
Lesson
Most of the freight saving came from load and lane design, not from negotiating rates.

Why I write this

I’ve spent close to a decade planning supply chains for beverage companies, from regional supply plans to national network and capacity decisions. Along the way I noticed that the most useful ideas rarely come from a single tool or textbook. They come from asking why something keeps going wrong, then testing a fix.

This site is my notebook for that. Each week I pick one question about planning, logistics or digitisation, read what the research says, and add what I’ve seen in practice. The models here are side projects I build to understand a problem properly. If something resonates, or you think I’ve got it wrong, I’d genuinely like to hear it.
